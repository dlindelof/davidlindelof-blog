I've always been involved in work with a research component to it. Even at Neurobat, where I spent most of my focus time working on developing and improving our algorithms. I've always found that that kind of work didn't lend itself well to the agile model of working in iteration (or at least, I have never been able to make it work). My career has since then developed into a full-time, individual contributor data scientist assigned to support several project teams, and I'm trying to figure out the best way to balance my commitments to each project.

To get some perspective I asked the following question on the [Rands Leadership Slack](https://randsinrepose.com/welcome-to-rands-leadership-slack/):

> Hi!  
>   
> I’m a data scientist at a large tech firm. The 16 members in my team report to the same manager. We are all assigned (by our manager) to different product teams that we support, but we are also frequently asked to carry out smaller side projects/analyses (possibly for other product teams), or to answer ad-hoc queries, say to help fill in the blanks in some exec’s upcoming presentation.  
>   
> Most of these projects take at least a full day to complete, sometimes much more. I frequently find myself working on 2-3 major projects in parallel, occasionally over more than a quarter, and dealing with smaller tasks that can last 1-2 weeks.  
>   
> So unlike, say, software engineering where work is ideally broken down to sprint-sized chunks, and where you rarely work for more than one stakeholder, our work sounds closer to that of a consultant, constantly working for several clients and trying to demonstrate progress to all of them.  
>   
> I’ve learned to avoid switching tasks in the same day and to allocate different days of the week to different projects. That lets me show regular progress to all my stakeholders, but I was wondering if anyone had better advice or could point me to resources on how to effectively work on multiple projects. 
> 
> [https://rands-leadership.slack.com/archives/C052PTMCA/p1647064207788229](https://rands-leadership.slack.com/archives/C052PTMCA/p1647064207788229)

The support and help I received were overwhelming. Many people chimed in, and I posted a follow-up summary of the advice I had received:

> Hi all! I just wanted to thank everyone who chimed in on my original question. Thanks for sharing the tips and tactics that worked for you. Here’s a summary of what I think will work in my situation:  
>   
> **Work in milestones:** for each project I’m involved in, set clear intermediate objectives (ideally doable in less than 2 calendar-weeks). If I’m developing a predictive model, the first milestone will be a naive, baseline model. The performance of this model may turn out to be sufficient.
> 
> **Maintain focus**: I don’t work on more than one project on a given day. I recognise the benefits of interleaving project work from day to day.
> 
> **Say no to less-impactful work:** my org’s performance review system emphasises impactful work. My manager supports saying no to work that won’t show up on my performance packet.
> 
> **Never agree immediately:** by replying “I need to run this past my manager” I buy myself time to think through the work item, and a plausible excuse for refusing the work.
> 
> **Overcommunicate:** few stakeholders will complain about too frequent status updates, especially in a post-work-from-office world.  
>   
> As we transition to Q2 I’ve reduced my in-flight projects from 7 to 3, ensuring I keep only projects that have impact potential and using my manager to decline (or reduce my commitment to) others.
> 
> [https://rands-leadership.slack.com/archives/C052PTMCA/p1649614952463169?thread\_ts=1647064207.788229&cid=C052PTMCA](https://rands-leadership.slack.com/archives/C052PTMCA/p1649614952463169?thread_ts=1647064207.788229&cid=C052PTMCA)

I don't expect this model to be the best solution; as I iterate on it I will keep you updated.
