title: "How I learned to stop worrying and love requirements documents"

---

There's a practice that's almost universally spurned by development teams. It's perceived as a relic of the waterfall system, a sure sign that the organization is degenerating into a bureaucratic glacier. So you might be surprised that I'm asking you today to give this practice a second chance: the requirements document.

At Google, teams are highly encouraged to write a so-called Products Requirements Document (PRD) for any new feature. These are typically 1-2 page lightweight documents with a summary of the background and the problem the feature aims to solve. Once the PRD is written it can be referred to by a Google Drive link (or, more commonly, a shortened link) in any bug report, commit message, or internal communication.

Writing requirements is hardly anathema in the world of agile software development. Mike Cohn in Succeding with Agile wrote that <find exact quote>. Leslie Lamport, author of LaTeX and <other systems>, is famously big on putting your thoughts down in writing because it forces you to, well, think. Joel Spolsky, in his [Joel test of the quality of a software team](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/08/09/the-joel-test-12-steps-to-better-code/), has "Do you have a spec?" at number 7 and famously quipped that "Writing specs is like flossing: everybody agrees that it’s a good thing, but nobody does it".

I'm all in favour of user stories scribbled on 3x5 cards; during my startup years I worked daily with nothing more than a vague idea of what needed to be done. And I'm not saying you should abandon the idea of 3x5 cards as the cradle of great ideas, but I would encourage you to insist that the PM/PO should complement the 3x5 card with a light, 1-2 page requirements document. It would not only give the team the necessary background to make sure they solve the real problem; it would also help regulate the flow of work, ensuring that bad ideas, once thought through, never hit the backlog.
