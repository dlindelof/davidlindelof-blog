---
title: "Legal protection of the \"Programmer\" title"
draft: true
---

The easiest, safest and most legal way to kill someone is to pretend you're a programmer when you are not.

Software is the only human artefact that can potentially cause physical or financial damage, or potentially kill people, whose production is not covered by any law in any country. Anyone can write code and be paid for it, no matter the end use.

Compare the situation with another lethal human artefact, say, electrical installations. It is illegal (at least in Switzerland) for a person with an electrician's diploma to make any modification to an electrical installation. You are not even supposed to carry out any sort of modification to your own home's electrical installations, however minor. Here is the text from the Geneva legislature:

> Seules ont le droit d’établir, de modifier ou de réparer une installation électrique à courant fort les personnes en possession d’une autorisation écrite, délivrée à cet effet par les Services industriels de Genève ou par l’inspection fédérale des installations à courant fort. (L 5 05.01: Règlement d’application de la loi sur les constructions et les installations diverses (RCI), Art. 91. Full text available here: http://www.ge.ch/legislation/rsg/f/rsg\_l5\_05p01.html)

which, loosely translated, means that no-one is allowed to build, modify or repair a strong-current installation (i.e. any electrical installation that can potentially kill someone) without the proper authorisation from the Geneva utility company or from the federal government.

But to obtain such authorisation you must hold an "Installateur-électricien CFC" diploma and have proved that you know your craft. Those diplomas are delivered, in Switzerland, under an apprenticeship model. Such an apprenticeship typically lasts three years.

As customers, we benefit immensely form such a legislature: when we commission someone to modify or repair an electrical installation, the work we pay for is vouched for by both the canton and the confederacy. If you use the services of an established electrical installer, it is very uncommon to have a sloppy work done.

Can the same be said of computer programs? Do we have such a guarantee when we pay someone to build, modify or repair a software system? Absolutely not. It's perfectly legal for anyone, without a programmer's diploma, to make any intervention on a software system.

Although there exists a legally protected "Informaticien CFC" title, I was unable to find any legal text permitting its holders to perform work on software systems. Or rather, there is nothing to prevent your average rookie from selling his services as IT Consultant.

You might argue that electrical installations are more dangerous than software systems. I beg to differ. You will easily find examples of historical software errors that have caused deaths, injuries, material damage, power outages or security breaches. One such list is [Wikipedia's list of software bugs](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_software_bugs).

We all know how much of our lives depend on the software running our computers, our cars, our homes, our planes, our power plants. Why then are these systems not legally protected?

I think what our industry is missing is something analogous to article 110.12 of the american National Electric Code (NEC):

> Electrical equipment shall be installed in a neat and workmanlike manner.

This statement is intentionally vague; it is expected that any professional should know what it means. In particular, if you are a professional electrical installer you should report to the proper authorities any installation that has obviously been carried out in a sloppy and careless manner.

How about having a similar rule for computer programmers?

> A computer shall be programmed following best practices.

It is time to raise the public's awareness about programming shops around the world that will hire any fresh graduates and have them contribute code to business-critical systems.
