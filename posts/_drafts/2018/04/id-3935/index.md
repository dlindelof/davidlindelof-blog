Gerald Weinberg's latest book about software testing is possibly the most important book on that topic I've read to date. Unlike most books about testing, which cover topics such as what should go in a test plan, equivalence classes, boundary conditions, and so on, Weinberg's book assumes you already know that material and discusses the testing activity at a much higher level.

The book seems intended for an organization that already has a solid testing culture, but who encounters the kind of difficulties common to all human societies: dealing with denial; interpreting and making sense of bug reports; learning about the organisation; and detecting what Weinberg calls testing scams.

Weinberg has been around the software industry for more than half a century. I believe I've read most of his books, and I find he's grown more assertive in his latest books, including this one. He's not shy about pointing out elements that signal serious flaws in an organisation, even when they mean the organisation is beyond helping.

On the negative side, the book suffers occasionally from mild inconsistencies of style. One chapter, for example, is written a story in an organisation and introduces characters, but the book never returns to that story. Four other chapters follow the communication model introduced by family therapist Virginia Satir (whom Weinberg frequently refers to in his books) and feel slightly out of place. But these are minor quibbles.

In short, this book is a perfect complement to the other classic books on testing out there. It will greatly amplify the effectiveness of your testing team. I highly recommend it.
