In the third week of May I travelled to Athens (Greece) together with our CEO to attend a three-day long workshop on the AMBASSADOR european project. AMBASSADOR, with its 15 partner organisations, aims at solving at district-level what we, at Neurobat, think have solved (or are solving) at the building level: eliminating energy waste by applying model-predictive control algorithms.

A large, distributed project such as this one is inherently risky. There are, however, a few things you can do right from the beginning that will minimize risk.

## Have a clear vision before having a clear strategy

A couple of years ago we were invited to the kick-off meeting of another similar project. There was a lot of enthusiasm in the meeting room; powerpoint followed powerpoint while each partner presented their idea of what the project was about. Then it hit me; I raised my hand and asked:

> Sorry, maybe I've missed something important but what is the vision of this project?

The room went silent for at least five seconds. Then the representative of one of the leading partners stammered some half-hearted statement, more or less paraphrasing the buzzword-rich statements that had been made in the project proposal. The project was killed a few months later.

In AMBASSADOR I have the feeling that everyone agrees what district-level management should look like, but there are traces of disagreement about what constitutes a district. The three test sites are very different in nature, and it is not clear whether they are really representative of what AMBASSADOR is about.

The sacred duty of a project coordinator is to ensure that everyone shares the same vision. Where there is ambiguity, he must resolve it. When someone gives a presentation that contradicts that vision, the coordinator must have the backbone to stand up and say so.

## Don't be overly ambitious (yet)

Unlike what's being offered by other brands, Neurobat has avoided developing a one-size-fits-all kind of device. Instead of controlling every aspect of the building, we control only one: the temperature of the heating fluid coming out of your boiler. Instead of trying to be compatible with every kind of building out there, our sweet spot is the single-family house.

When I first got involved in AMBASSADOR, the project description contained several keywords that I have come to be wary of, such as "holistic", "integrate", "generic", etc. I am skeptical of any attempt at developing a control system that does "everything" under the sun. I usually translate these buzzwords as "all-singing, all-dancing".

Good software engineers recognise holisticism when it rears its ugly head in the system they develop. It goes by various names; "God module", "God class" or "God function" is a frequently heard one. It is considered bad engineering practice, for such a module/class/function is very susceptible to change: since it handles so many things, many things can require the engineer to change it. Call this a consequence of the Single Responsibility Principle.
